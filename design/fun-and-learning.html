Fun · 1

A small skill, without fast satellites

Tracking is out because satellites drift slowly. These ask for timing, judgement or knowledge instead.

RecommendedCatch the fade

When a satellite is about to slide into Earth's shadow, a thin ring counts down around the circle. Tap in the last 3 seconds before it vanishes for a shiny. Too early: a normal card. Too late: it's gone until next pass.

Real timing skill, it's a lovely moment to watch, and it teaches the shadow. Builds on the eclipse shiny we already detect.

RecommendedName that light

Before a new card is revealed, guess what it is from the way it looks: steady (satellite), flashing or tumbling (rocket stage), or faint and fast (debris). Right guess: +10 XP and a "Sharp eye" streak.

Teaches people to read the sky. One tap, no slowdown.

Call the rise

By day, for a pass tonight, drag a marker to where you think it will come up over the horizon. At the pass, the closer you were, the more XP.

Teaches orbits and directions. Slower; better as a weekly challenge.

Faint is worth more

A catch near your sky's limit (only just visible) earns a "Hard catch" bonus, shown as a small diamond on the card.

Rewards dark sites and binoculars. No new UI, but less of a moment.

Fun · 5

More secrets

Same rules as the others: never mentioned, a patch when found. Ones tied to real dates and real history teach something too.

Sputnik's beep

Every 4 October (launch day, 1957), a ghost Sputnik crosses the evening sky, beeping. Tap it: "Beep-beep" patch.

The lost tool bag

In 2008 an astronaut let go of a tool bag on a spacewalk and amateurs tracked it for months. A tiny bag drifts near the ISS on one random pass a month.

Ed White's glove

Lost on the first American spacewalk (1965). A glove tumbles across the sky very rarely, by day only.

The Arecibo message

Hold the circle on the Hercules Cluster (M13) for 10 s and the 1974 Arecibo radio message draws itself, pixel by pixel. "Hello from Earth".

Halley's way home

Aim where Halley's Comet really is now (far out in Hydra). "Coming back in 2061" and a countdown patch.

Hold still on Polaris

Keep Polaris in the circle for 60 s and the stars start wheeling around it, sped up. "Pole Star" patch.

Apollo 11's footprints

On 20 July, line up the Moon and a marker appears on the Sea of Tranquility. "One small step".

Laika

On 3 November, Sputnik 2's ghost crosses with a small paw print. A gentle story card about the first animal in orbit.

An old Iridium flare

The original Iridium satellites flashed brighter than Venus; the last flare was in 2019. Once in a long while at dusk, a phantom flare: tap it while it's bright.

Two stations

When the ISS and Tiangong are both up at once (it happens a few times a year), catch both within 10 minutes: "Two Homes" patch.

The space jellyfish

A rocket launch at dusk lights its plume like a jellyfish. When a real launch happens near twilight, a glowing jellyfish shape appears where it would be.

Night owl's hour

Open the app between 3 and 4 am and point straight up: a tiny owl sits on the meridian. "3 AM Club".

Learning · 3 · where and how

Earth's shadow

Satellites only shine while sunlight reaches them. After dusk, Earth's shadow rises up the sky from the east; anything inside it goes dark. Three places this can show:

SPACE COLLECTOR
EARTH'S SHADOW · 400 KM UP
EXPLORECOLLECTION

1 · Recommended. In the sky at dusk and dawn: a soft dark band where the shadow is (at the height most satellites fly). You see things blink out as they cross it.

WHY IS NOTHING UP?
At 1 am you're deep in Earth's shadow, and so is everything low overhead. Satellites light up again about 2 hours before sunrise.

2 · A short explainer, opened from the Now tab when it's empty in the middle of the night ("Why is nothing up?").

FADES IN 4 S · TAP AS IT GOES
EXPLORECOLLECTION

3 · With "Catch the fade": the countdown ring around the circle as it reaches the shadow.
